## Poster #1
**Title:** Design and application of small molecules for the interception of quorum sensing in Staphylococcus aureus\
**Authors:** Clay, A.P.; Polaske, T.J.P.; Blackwell, H.E.B.\
**Presenter:** Alyssa Clay; Chemistry\
**Abstract:** Quorum sensing (QS) is the process by which bacteria of the same species coordinate behavior, including virulence, at a high population density. The accessory gene regulator (agr) QS system found in the Gram-positive pathogen Staphylococcus aureus uses agr to regulate an arsenal of toxins and biofilm formation. Small molecules that inhibit agr QS have the potential to be used as therapeutics to combat S. aureus infection. Previous small molecules that have been reported to inhibit agr QS suffer from low potencies, poorly characterized mechanisms of action, and/or associated toxicities that render them impractical for use as chemical probes. We have developed a new class of small molecule agr inhibitors, and are studying their biochemical mechanisms of agr inhibition. I have designed a new synthetic route to the core structure of a best-in-class small molecule inhibitor, which is the most potent small molecule inhibitor of S. aureus agr QS to date. This synthesis enables facile functional group variation for the delineation of structure activity relationship analyses to improve compound potency, membrane permeability, solubility, and stability. Characterization of the mechanism of agr inhibition by this new small molecule scaffold is currently underway. Almost all known agr inhibitors target autoinducer (AIP) binding to the transmembrane histidine kinase AgrC or intracellular response regulator AgrA:DNA binding. Elucidating the mechanisms of agr inhibition by these new small molecules may provide access to chemical probes that target other components of the agr QS system. It has been demonstrated that the best-in-class small molecule inhibitor is a competitive inhibitor of AIP:AgrC binding, although the exact mechanism of inhibition remains unknown.\

## Poster #2
**Title:** Effects of Psychosocial Stress on Laryngeal Biology: Implications for how our Voice sounds\
**Authors:** Anumitha Venkatraman, Katelyn Jacobs, John Binns, Susan Thibeault\
**Presenter:** Anumitha Venkatraman; Surgery/Otolaryngology Head and Neck\
**Abstract:** Background: Psychosocial stress and voice production are inextricably linked. Psychosocial stress can change the pitch/intensity of our voice, or cause significant muscle tension in the throat (causing hoarseness). However, the biological relationship between stress and the voice is unknown.  Objective: To shed light on the cellular and microbiological mechanisms of stress in the larynx (voice box). Three targeted mechanisms were studied:  1)	How stress affects the laryngeal microbiota? Stress is known to play a role in gut dysbiosis, thereby regulating changes in social behavior via the microbial gut-brain axis. If replicated in the larynx, it may provide a biological basis for stress-induced voice changes found in anecdotal evidence.  2)	How stress affects vocal fold epithelial integrity? Stress-altered decreases in intestinal epithelial barrier integrity leads to inflammation and increases infection risk. If replicated in the larynx, it could increase susceptibility to environmental (pollutants), or systemic (acid reflux) elements.  3)	How stress affects laryngeal sensory mechanisms? Stress is reported in chronic cough. If stress alters laryngeal sensory mechanisms, it could lead to increased laryngeal hypersensitivity associated with chronic cough. Methods: 16S rRNAseq, qPCR, immunofluorescence were employed in stressed and unstressed C56BL/7 mice (N=108 total) Results/Discussion: 1)	 Stress results in laryngeal dysbiosis (characterized by changes in alpha, beta diversity). Within genera-specific changes, there was an increase in pathogenic-bacteria Streptococcus, decrease in Corynebacterium.  2)	Stress results in decreased epithelial barrier integrity; increased gene expression of inflammatory cytokines, decreased protein expression of mucins, tight junctions, indicating stress-induced increase in laryngeal susceptibility to noxious elements. 3)	Stress alters gene and protein expression of laryngeal sensory receptors, indicating potential for increased laryngeal hypersensitivity. Takeaways: There is a biological basis for the relationship between stress and voice production via different cellular and microbial mechanisms. Future research can relate stress-related voice symptomology to biological underpinnings in the larynx.\

## Poster #3
**Title:** Designing Autonomous, Conversational Robots to Support Remote Space Exploration\
**Authors:** Yaxin Hu*, Arissa J. Sato*, Jingxin Du, Pragathi Praveena, Bilge Mutlu (*authors of equal contribution)\
**Presenter:** Arissa Sato; Department of Computer Sciences\
**Abstract:** In this work, we explore how AI-enabled conversational capabilities on a mobile robot can facilitate exploration of a remote space. We prototyped a robot system that enables users to navigate, ask questions about, and receive recommendations for ways to experience a remote environment. We deployed our prototype to provide a remote tour of a geology museum and asked 11 remote participants to use the robot. The study uncovered user patterns that point to design considerations for the user's remote experience with exhibits; their thoughts on the social space provided via the robot; the robot's ability to suggest and navigate touring routes for users; and suggestions on how to design and convey the robot's knowledge, manners, and behavior. Our work highlights the potential for autonomous, conversation capabilities to provide users with rich remote experiences and offers design guidelines on these robots.\

## Poster #5
**Title:** Machine Learning for Data Integrity: Validating Rural Property Identities in the Brazilian Amazon to End Deforestation\
**Authors:** Cain√£ M. Couto-Silva, Matthew Christie, Lisa Rausch, Holly K. Gibbs\
**Presenter:** Cain√£ Max Couto da Silva; Department of Geography\
**Abstract:** Brazil continues to have the world's highest rates of deforestation and most efforts aiming to address this focus on individual properties and producers. Over the last 15 years, we have focussed on addressing deforestation in cattle supply chains by collecting and integrating land use, sales, and environmental data about these properties from multiple sources. This is challenging due to the variations in names and attributes for the same rural property across different data sources, which led us to implement a matching algorithm that labels observations referring to the same property, assigning each group a unique property group identifier (PGI). However, given the large amount of data (> 15 million records and >3 million PGIs), there has been no easy way to validate the data integrity of PGIs to date. To address this,  we aimed to train a classifier using machine learning to predict whether a PGI is ill-formed or not (i.e., whether or not all properties labeled to the same PGI refer to the exact property unit), enabling us to validate the integrity of PGIs quickly and reliably. Based on domain knowledge and manual inspection, we annotated the labels for 100 representative PGIs using a stratified random sampling approach, stratifying by PGI group size. As part of our data pipeline, we prepared the modeling data by aggregating the property data into a PGI-granularity level, computing custom and descriptive statistics as features for each attribute. Additionally, as part of our feature-engineering process, we've applied DBSCAN to calculate the distinct count of property clusters based on geolocation data. We've used the annotated labels and prepared features to train rule-based and machine-learning models to predict our target. We validated the model using cross-validation and bootstrapping. We chose a Catboost model tuned to deal with unbalanced targets as our final model since it outperformed our baseline models, reaching an average of 91% accuracy, 83% recall, and 84% precision. We have also developed a web app to inspect raw and aggregated data, red flags, and geolocation maps for each target PGI.\

## Poster #6
**Title:** Microbial investigations of coral algal symbionts exposed to stony coral tissue loss disease reveal unseen diversity\
**Authors:** Charlotte E. Ford, Christopher D. Dunn, Caroline Dennison, Andrew Baker, Richard Karp and Tony L. Goldberg\
**Presenter:** Charlotte Ford; Pathbiological sciences\
**Abstract:** Stony coral tissue loss disease (SCTLD) affects scleractinian (stony) corals (order Scleractinia), causing a rapid onset of tissue loss which can result in complete colony mortality over a timescale of months. It is estimated that at least 22 stony coral species are affected by SCTLD. SCTLD was first observed off the coast of Miami, Florida in 2014 and has since spread to reefs in 30 countries/territories across the Caribbean. The etiological agent of SCTLD has not yet been identified but is believed to be infectious. Recent research suggests that algal symbionts may be among the first to show signs of disruption which then leads to rapid degradation of coral tissues. Our aim was to identify an infectious agent(s) which could have a role in the etiology of SCTLD. We investigated the microbial composition of cultured symbiodinium dinoflagellates (family Symbiodiniaceae; n=12) experimentally exposed to SCTLD. Breviolum minitum, Durusdinium trenchii and Cladocopium goreaui were exposed to unfiltered or filtered (0.2ÔÅ≠m) water from a prior SCTLD infection and sampled before exposure, pre-disease and during disease. We performed metagenomic sequencing for virus discovery and 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing (V4 region) for microbiome analysis. Bacterial composition differed significantly between symbiont species (p = 0.0348) but not treatment group. Overall viral diversity was dominated by bacteriophage (72%), with diversity most affected by symbiont species (p = <0.0001) and treatment group (p = 0.01253). We identified 36 novel viruses in this study; 10 were found to be present only in the symbiodinium exposed to SCTLD. No virus or bacteria were found to be significantly enriched in samples exhibiting disease or indeed those exposed to SCTLD. Whilst no single pathogen was associated with SCTLD, novel microbial diversity within coral algal symbionts described in this study will be valuable for future studies.\

## Poster #7
**Title:** RNAseq reveals distinct mechanosensitive landscape underlying arrhythmogenicity of the superior vena cava\
**Authors:** Turner DGP, Egorov YuV, Glukhov AV\
**Presenter:** Daniel Turner; Medicine\
**Abstract:** Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common heart rhythm disorder and increases the risk of death. AF arises from aberrant ion channel activity, which alters cardiac electrophysiology and induces arrhythmic irregular heartbeats. AF often occurs during hypertension and importantly, arises from discrete vulnerable regions of the atria located near veins where blood enters the heart, including the superior vena cava (SVC). However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms that render these regions more susceptible to arrythmia is poorly understood. To determine molecular mechanisms of region-specific mechanosensitivity and hypertension-induced pathological remodeling, we utilized bulk RNA-sequencing of 12-months-old normotensive (n=4) and spontaneously hypertensive (n=4) rat SVC and RA tissue samples to compare expression of >10,000 mRNAs. Region-specific and hypertension-induced differences were linked to signaling pathways relevant to cardiac electrophysiology, contraction, and mechanosensitivity. First, we  found significantly altered expressions of 11 mechanosensitive ion channels in the SVC, some of which are implicated in cardiac arrythmia including KCNMA1 and TRPV4. Secondly, in the SVC we found 22 significantly altered mRNA expressions that may affect cardiac contraction and alter ion channel activity, such as PKA- and SERCA-associated mRNAs. Lastly, we observed that 23 mRNA related to fibrosis were significantly changed in the SVC including various collagen mRNAs. As changes in the extracellular matrix could affect the stiffness and thus mechano-sensitivity of the heart, these fibrotic changes may increase the hypersensitivity of the SVC to mechanical stretch during hypertension as observed in electrophysiological studies. In both hypertensive SVC and RA, we observed changes in mechanosensitive, cytoskeletal, and inflammatory mRNA expression but less than hypothesized. We suspect that altered protein activity and/or post-translational modifications may play a more important role in mediating hypertension-induced arrhythmogenesis in the SVC.  In conclusion, our RNA-sequencing data confirms that the SVC has significantly different expressions of various mechano-responsive mRNA, compared to RA, which may underly the hypersensitivity of the SVC to mechanical stretch and arrythmia generation.\

## Poster #8
**Title:** Effects of NREM sleep on the cortical synaptic expression of GluA1-containing AMPA receptors\
**Authors:** Fabio Squarcio, Giulio Tononi, Chiara Cirelli\
**Presenter:** Fabio Squarcio; Department of Psychiatry\
**Abstract:** A net increase in synaptic strength accrues in many synapses during wakefulness as the inevitable consequence of ongoing learning. However, to avoid saturation and keep energy costs under control, the wake-related synaptic strengthening needs to be corrected via a broad but selective process of synaptic weakening. Sleep seems to promote this net decrease in excitatory synaptic strength. Nonetheless, several outstanding questions about sleep-dependent synaptic weakening remain. A major unresolved question relates to whether non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep play specific roles in sleep-dependent synaptic weakening. Another unresolved question is whether a few hours of recovery sleep following acute sleep deprivation are sufficient to bring net synaptic strength back to baseline levels.  In order to address these questions, we used two established molecular markers of synaptic strength, the levels of the AMPA (Alpha-Amino-3-Hydroxy-5-Methyl-4-Isoxazole Propionic Acid) receptors containing the GluA1 subunit, and the phosphorylation of GluA1 at serine 845 (p-GluA1(845)). We measured changes of these two markers in synaptosomes of mouse cortex (one hemisphere), using the following 4 experimental conditions (8 C57BL/B6 mice per condition, 14-15 weeks old of both sexes): sleep (5 hours), sleep deprivation (5 hours, with novel objects), recovery sleep (5 hours) after sleep deprivation (5 hours), and REM sleep deprivation (5 hours). Finally, we also tested whether sleep-dependent synaptic weakening is affected by aging. For this experiment we used aged mice (85-86 weeks old) assigned to 3 experimental conditions: sleep, sleep deprivation and recovery sleep after sleep deprivation (4 male mice per condition). We found that the renormalization of GluA1 and p-GluA1(845) expression seems to crucially rely on NREM sleep and can occur in a few hours of sleep after acute sleep deprivation. Moreover, this process seems to be unaffected by aging.\

## Poster #10
**Title:** A cross-system functional comparison of primary succession in bacteria\
**Authors:** Cagle, Grace A.; Bernardin, Jessica; Baiser, Benjamin; Bittleston, Leonora; Young, Erica; Gray, Sarah M.; Freedman, Zachary B.\
**Presenter:** Grace Cagle; Soil and Environmental Sciences\
**Abstract:** Primary succession is a dramatic example of functional change over time. Theory developed from plant ecology predicts that interspecific competition drives the replacement of weedy, fast-growing colonizers with slower-growing species, promoting the accumulation of carbon and nutrients often observed in mature ecosystems. But does primary succession in bacterial communities follow a similar trajectory? Identifying generalizable principles to describe microbial community function during primary succession will facilitate the development of a more holistic theory of ecological succession. We classified bacterial communities into trait-based groups and then determined how the abundances of these groups changed during primary succession. We hypothesized that three groups would best fit the data and that the groups' abundances would change over time according to the availability of carbon, nutrient supplies, and accumulation of metabolic byproducts in the system. Data were obtained via a systematic review of metagenomics-based studies on primary succession in bacteria following the PRISMA protocol. The studies were screened for inclusion, and the functional potential of each metagenome was estimated from normalized gene copy numbers. Functional genes were selected a priori to represent each trait-based group. Additionally, we evaluated which traits contributed to sample groups classified using an unsupervised topic modeling approach on the full suite of functional genes. To determine how the abundances of the trait-based groups changed over time and across systems, their temporal trajectories were mapped on ternary plots and evaluated using random forest analysis. Initial results showed a consistent pattern in functional primary succession in systems with limited organic carbon, supporting the hypothesis that carbon availability is a predictive factor. These initial findings indicate bacterial life-history strategies change during primary succession and are associated with physiological trade-offs. Forthcoming results will reveal how the trajectories of ecological groups, and the traits that define them, differ across systems.\

## Poster #11
**Title:** Improved Monitoring of Alfalfa Yield and Quality from Multisource Optical and SAR Satellite Data: Ensuring the Security of Dairy Products\
**Authors:** Jiang Chen, Zhou Zhang\
**Presenter:** Jiang Chen; Department of Biological Systems Engineering\
**Abstract:** Precision agriculture management and practices using satellite remote sensing images play an important role for monitoring crop growth under the context of climate change. Alfalfa is a vital forage crop for various livestock and widely distributed around the world. Satellite remote sensing has been broadly employed to monitor corn and soybean. However, unlike corn and soybean, alfalfa is usually monthly harvested and has a rapid regrowth stage, and thus its growth is difficult to describe using a traditional phenological curve. Limited by the availability of high spatio-temporal resolution images from single satellite, few studies devoted to monitoring fine-scale (e.g., <= 30-m) alfalfa growth using satellite images. Fusion of Landsat/Sentinel-2 optical and Sentinel-1 SAR images can enhance the resolution of satellite data. To form a unified optical and SAR image from multisource satellites, there are still four specific issues during satellite image fusion: (1) Landsat has no red edge band; (2) the effects of solar angles on optical data fusion have not been evaluated and corrected; (3) climate data have not been used for generating more accurate optical vegetation indices (VIs) from SAR data; (4) the feasibility of optical-based SAR fusion has not been explored. To address these issues, this study proposes a new multisource optical and SAR data fusion framework with Landsat-7/8, Sentinel-2, and Sentinel-1 satellites for monitoring fine-scale alfalfa growth. The data fusion framework includes three models, i.e., Landsat2Sentinel-2, SAR2Optical, and Optical2SAR. Using random forest (RF) algorithm and the known spectral bands, the Landsat2Sentinel-2 model improves Landsat-based red edge reflectance fusion (RMSE reduced 28.22-31.16%) compared to multiple linear regression (MLR). Furthermore, RMSE was reduced by 22.61-23.58% if considering solar angles in the fusion model. Compared to the traditional linear regression model, Landsat2Sentinel-2 model more accurately fuses Landsat and Sentinel-2 derived four surface VIs. The SAR2Optical model improves VIs fusion (RMSE reduced 32.18-34.59%) since climate data were considered. The Optical2SAR fusion model can accurately produce optical-based SAR data, demonstrating that optical and SAR satellite data can be reciprocally fused. Moreover, the developed data fusion models present improved performance for fusing optical data at both surface and top-of-atmosphere levels. On the other hand, the new methodological framework can be employed to develop a unified data fusion model across different crop types. Overall, this study proposes a new multisource optical and SAR data fusion framework which has great implications for ensuring the security of dairy products.\

## Poster #12
**Title:** Computational Discovery of Fast Interstitial Oxygen Conductors\
**Authors:** Jun Meng, Md Sariful Sheikh, Ryan Jacobs, Jian Liu, William O. Nachlas, Xiangguo Li, Dane Morgan\
**Presenter:** Jun Meng; Materials Science and Engineering\
**Abstract:** Oxygen-active materials are crucial for a variety of applications such as solid-oxide fuel cells, gas sensors, catalysts, and oxygen separation membranes. Currently, the predominant families of oxygen-active materials used in commercial devices often diffuse oxygen via a vacancy mechanism (such as perovskites and fluorites), and only have adequate oxygen diffusivity and surface exchange at high temperatures (e.g., ~800 ÀöC for fuel cells), resulting in high system cost and materials degradation. Interstitial oxygen conductors generally have lower migration barriers compared to vacancy conductors, which, in turn, can provide orders of magnitude kinetic enhancement at lower temperatures. In this work, we combined physically-motivated structure and property descriptors, ab initio simulations, and experiments to demonstrate an approach to discover new fast interstitial oxygen conductors.[1] We identified multiple new promising structural classes, and demonstrated the exceptional performance of one example material, La4Mn5Si4O22+ùúπ (LMS) with experimental validation. Experimental studies uncovered an interstitial oxygen content of Œ¥=0.42 in as-synthesized LMS, as determined by electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA) analysis. Ionic conductivity measurements demonstrate that LMS exhibits considerably higher conductivity than widely used commercial materials such as LSCF and YSZ, and comparable or improved conductivity compared to other known interstitial oxygen conductors. The fast oxygen ion conduction is attributed to the simultaneously active interstitial and interstitialcy diffusion pathways revealed by ab initio studies. Electrical conductivity relaxation (ECR) studies have shown that LMS has among the highest k_chem values at low temperatures of any known material, suggesting LMS has the potential to facilitate fast oxygen reduction kinetics, for example, by reducing area specific resistance in the air electrode of solid oxide cells for electricity or hydrogen production. LMS is just one member of multiple new families of promising fast interstitial oxygen conductors identified in this work, suggesting that many more materials exhibiting fast interstitial oxygen kinetics remain to be found. We show that all known interstitial oxygen conductors have two enabling features in common: (i) availability of electrons for oxygen reduction and (ii) structural flexibility enabling sufficient accessible volume. We propose that these are two essential features to effectively incorporate and diffuse interstitial oxygen, which provides guidance for future discovery and analysis of interstitial oxygen diffusing materials. ## Poster #14
**Title:** 2D IR of voltage gating in potassium ion channels\
**Authors:** Kiera B Wilhelm, Matthew J Ryan, Kyle R Billings, Lujia Gao, Francis I Valiyaveetil, Alexei A Kananenka, Martin T Zanni\
**Presenter:** Kiera Wilhelm; Chemistry\
**Abstract:** Membrane proteins respond to the potential across cell membranes to regulate many basic cellular functions. One quintessential class of these proteins, voltage-gated potassium ion channels (Kv), regulates potassium ion efflux to reestablish the resting potential of cells after membrane depolarization. Kv channels contain a voltage-sensing domain (VSD) that senses local membrane potential and drives a conformational change to regulate opening and closing of the pore domain. While there exist many high-resolution structures of Kv channels in depolarized states, new methods are needed to experimentally probe Kv structure under applied potentials and to map the mechanism of voltage gating. We have recently developed a new means of investigating structural dynamics of membrane proteins under an electric field using surface-enhanced two-dimensional infrared (2D IR) spectroscopy. Using residue-specific, environmentally-sensitive cyano-tags, we probe voltage-dependent structural changes between the hyperpolarized and depolarized states of the VSD of the model archaeal channel KvAP. Steered molecular dynamics simulations of membrane-embedded KvAP VSD under a series of transmembrane potentials, using the crystal structure of KvAP VSD in its depolarized state as the starting structure, are used to interpret spectral features. Further work developing transient 2D IR to time-resolve the gating motion in response to a rapid voltage pulse will be discussed. By investigating protein structural dynamics under the influence of an electric field, our 2D IR measurements connect classical electrophysiology techniques that measure current with structural biology techniques that provide structure. This method can be extended to broadly study protein structure and dynamics at cellular resting potentials.\

## Poster #15
**Title:** Strategies and Challenges For Engaging Latine Older Adults in Physical Activity in Wisconsin\
**Authors:** Laura Prieto, Kristen Pickett, Luis Columna\
**Presenter:** Laura Prieto; Kinesiology/ Family Medicine & Community Health\
**Abstract:** Participation in physical activity (PA) can reduce the risk of disability, maintain quality of life, and promote independence for older adults. Facilitators to PA participation for older adults include having community leaders facilitate evidence-based programming at senior centers and health care professionals recommending PA. Yet, racial and ethnic disparities still persist with Latine older adults reporting lower levels of PA than white older adults. Thus, the purpose of this descriptive qualitative study was to describe the experiences of senior center staff, program instructors, community health workers, and health care professionals in engaging Latine older adults in PA. Senior center staff and community health workers (n = 11) and health care professionals ( n = 6 ) working in the state of Wisconsin were purposively recruited for this study. Data were collected using demographic questionnaires and semi-structured interviews, which were transcribed and analyzed using thematic line-by-line analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Three themes were constructed from the data, (a) Creando Confianza (Creating Trust), (b) Meeting Latino Older Adults Where They Are, and (c) Make it or Break it: The Contrasting Levels of Institutional Support. Facilitators to PA included connecting with the Latine members outside of community programs, hosting PA programs at familiar geographical locations, and progressing PA based on health needs. Barriers to PA included not knowing how to individualize PA recommendations, lack of administrative support, and lack of adequate transportation. Findings inform strategies to improve reach and adoption of a future PA intervention for Latine older adults.\

**Authors:** Liza Loza, Thomas Hurtaux, Elizabeth Gaylord, Tamara Doering\
**Presenter:** Liza Loza; Genetics\
**Abstract:** The fungal cell wall (CW) protects the cell from external stressors, including heat, salt, pH changes, and nitrosative and oxidative stress. This complex structure is composed of interconnected glycans including chitin, variably linked glucans, and mannan. In pathogenic fungi, defects in CW synthesis often result in loss of thermotolerance and hence virulence. Echinocandin drugs, which target Œ≤-1,3-glucan synthesis, exploit this weakness. Despite the obvious importance of the CW to fungal survival in the host, many basic questions remain about its synthesis. Œ≤-1,6-glucan is the predominant glucan species in the CW of the pathogenic yeast Cryptococcus neoformans and is critical for connecting other CW glycans to each other. While some cryptococcal proteins are known to affect Œ≤-1,6-glucan abundance, the actual synthet-ic machinery has never been identified. We have identified a candidate gene whose product, a putative in-verting glycosyltransferase, may participate in Œ≤-1,6-glucan synthesis. The corresponding deletion mutant is sensitive to temperature and Congo red pigment in vitro. Consistent with a loss of thermotolerance, it is avirulent in a mouse model of infection. Enzymatic analysis of mutant CW material reveals a dramatic reduction in Œ≤-1,6-glucan relative to WT, potentially implicating this protein in the synthesis of this im-portant CW component. Experiments to define the purified protein's enzymatic activity and product are ongoing. We aim to fill gaps in our knowledge of fungal CW synthesis by elucidating the cellular and en-zymatic roles of this glycosyltransferase, which is clearly crucial for CW integrity in C. neoformans.\

## Poster #19
**Title:** Toxicity Evaluations of Botulinum Neurotoxin A1 Mutants Designed to be Atoxic\
**Authors:** Polrit Viravathana, William H. Tepp, Marite Bradshaw, Amanda Przedpelski, Joseph T. Barbieri, and Sabine Pellett\
**Presenter:** Polrit Viravathana; Bacteriology\
**Abstract:** Recombinant mutant holotoxin BoNTs (rBoNTs) are being evaluated as possible vaccines against botulism. Previously, several rBoNTs containing 2-3 amino acid mutations in the light chain (LC) showed significant decreases in toxicity (2.5‚Äì12.5 million-fold) versus wildtype BoNT/A1, leading to their current exclusion from the Federal Select Agent list. This report demonstrates the impact of production systems on residual toxicity of genetically inactivated rBoNTs.\

## Poster #20
**Title:** Defining the role of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) in Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) pathogenesis\
**Authors:** Quincy Rosemarie, Mitch Hayes, Eric Johannsen\
**Presenter:** Quincy Rosemarie; Oncology (McArdle Laboratory for Cancer Research)\
**Abstract:** Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is both the most common and aggressive lymphoma. DLBCL is not a single disease, but a group of histologically similar yet genetically distinct cancers with unique prognostic and therapeutic features. The recently defined genetic landscape of DLBCL includes at least seven subtypes, but does not consider Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status, a human tumor virus, despite up to 14% of DLBCL being EBV-positive and EBV's known role in B cell lymphomagenesis. We have reanalyzed published DLBCL genetic data and found that ~1/3 of EBV-positive DLBCLs are BN2 subtype (named for frequent BCL6 and Notch2 mutations). The remaining 2/3 of EBV-positive DLBCLs appear to be distinct from currently defined subtypes. Our preliminary findings indicate that: 1) EBV-positive DLBCL, like DLBCL itself, is not a single disease, and 2) EBV-positive and BN2 DLBCLs likely have a shared pathogenesis. Our analyses of mutations in EBV-positive vs EBV-negative DLBCL within the BN2 subtype revealed several genes frequently mutated in EBV-negative BN2 tumors that are not mutated in their EBV-positive counterparts. These genes include subtype-defining features, B cell receptor signaling genes, cell cycle regulators, and genes known to be downstream of EBV, thus supporting the hypothesis that EBV confers oncogenic properties to these tumors by functionally compensating for cellular mutations that drive cancer. We are extending our study to define the genetic landscape of EBV-positive DLBCLs using genomic and transcriptomic analyses, genetically characterize existing cell line models of EBV-positive DLBCL, and perform CRISPR screening to functionally characterize the role of EBV in BN2 DLBCL pathogenesis. Integrating EBV status into the classification and study of DLBCL will enhance our understanding of the biology of this disease, resolve the specific role(s) of EBV in different DLBCL subtypes, and inform the development of DLBCL models and targeted therapies.\

## Poster #22
**Title:** The role of phenotypic plasticity in the adaptive capacity of fish and wildlife species to climate change in the Midwestern United States\
**Authors:** Sarah Ramirez, Benjamin Zuckerberg, Jake Vander Zanden, and Dan Vimont\
**Presenter:** Sarah Ramirez; Center for Limnology; and, Forest and Wildlife Ecology\
**Abstract:** Many species display unique phenotypes (observable characteristics or traits) that align with specific environmental cues associated with favorable climate conditions. In a rapidly changing climate, however, many organisms face situations where phenotypes no longer align well with novel climatic conditions, resulting in a phenotypic mismatch. Phenotypic mismatches could induce significant demographic costs. For example, snowshoe hares (Lepus americanus) rely on day length to initiate molting to change their coat color from brown to white to match snow cover, but the widespread loss of snow cover is causing a mismatch. Phenotypic plasticity is one pathway by which individuals may adjust their phenotypes by responding to environmental cues that may more accurately predict favorable conditions. For this project, we will synthesize existing literature to identify managerially important species that exhibit climate-sensitive phenotypes to identify potential phenotypic mismatches. Working with a group of federal, tribal, and state partners, we will identify species of management, conservation, or cultural interest to investigate the role of phenotypic variation and plasticity in adaptive capacity of those species to climate change. We will strive to identify potential phenotypic and phenological mismatches during years of extremely variable weather patterns, followed by investigating the role of phenotypic plasticity in minimizing the risks of these mismatches, and determining the mechanisms behind why those mismatches are detrimental to persistence of populations. As climate change progresses, knowledge about the ability of populations to respond and adjust to predicted extreme variability is important for anticipating management actions needed to maintain those populations.\
